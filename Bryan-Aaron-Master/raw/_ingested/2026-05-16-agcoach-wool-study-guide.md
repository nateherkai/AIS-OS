# A Comprehensive Guide to Wool and Mohair Evaluation

This study guide provides an exhaustive overview of the principles and practices involved in judging wool and mohair. It details the economic significance, grading systems, evaluation criteria, and scoring methodologies used in professional and educational livestock judging contexts.

## The Economic Importance of Wool and Mohair

In the United States, Texas serves as a primary hub for fiber production, generating approximately 20 percent of the total wool clip and more than 97 percent of the total mohair clip. These products contribute an estimated $50 million annually to the state's agricultural income. For producers, knowledge of evaluation and grading is essential for:
*   Calculating the true market value of products.
*   Executing breeding programs to produce popular and profitable fiber types.
*   Ensuring yarn uniformity by producing consistent fiber lengths and fineness.

## Part 1: Wool Evaluation and Grading

Wool is produced by approximately 20 popular sheep breeds and various crossbreds. Evaluation is based on several key characteristics that determine manufacturing suitability and market price.

### 1. Grading Systems
Wool is graded primarily based on **fineness** (fiber diameter) and **length**.

#### The American Grade System
There are six standard grades based on fineness. These names do not indicate the specific breeding of the sheep:
1.  Fine
2.  1/2 blood
3.  3/8 blood
4.  1/4 blood
5.  Low 1/4 blood
6.  Braid

#### The Numerical (Bradford Spinning Count) System
This system is more accurate and widely used in the trade. The "spinning count" is the maximum spinning capacity of the wool, determined by the number of "hanks" weighing one pound. A worsted hank contains 560 yards of yarn.

| American Grade | Spinning Count | Micron Range |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Fine | 64’s, 70’s, 80’s | <17.70 – 22.04 |
| 1/2 blood | 60’s, 62’s | 22.05 – 24.94 |
| 3/8 blood | 56’s, 58’s | 24.95 – 27.84 |
| 1/4 blood | 50’s, 54’s | 27.85 – 30.99 |
| Low 1/4 blood | 46’s, 48’s | (N/A) |
| Braid | 36’s, 40’s, 44’s | (N/A) |

### 2. Length Classifications
Length is measured from the base to the tip of the unstretched fiber. Within each fineness grade, wool is categorized into three length grades:
*   **Staple:** The longest wools within a grade, suitable for Noble combs; brings the highest price.
*   **French Combing:** Medium-length wools that can be combed on French combs.
*   **Clothing:** Short-stapled wools unsuitable for combing, used in the woolen manufacturing system; brings the lowest price.

**Length Requirements (Inches)**
| Grade (Fineness) | Clothing | French Combing | Staple |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Fine | 2 and under | 2 – 3 | 3 and over |
| 1/2 blood | 2 1/2 and under | 2 1/2 – 3 1/2 | 3 1/2 and over |
| 3/8 blood | Under 3 1/4 | (Dropped*) | 3 1/4 and over |
| 1/4 blood | Under 4 | (Dropped*) | 4 and over |
| Low 1/4 blood | Under 4 1/4 | (Dropped*) | 4 1/4 and over |
| Braid | Under 4 1/2 | (Dropped*) | 4 1/2 and over |
*\*In coarser grades, the French combing classification is typically dropped.*

### 3. Yield and Shrinkage
*   **Shrinkage:** The percentage of foreign material (dirt, sand, vegetable matter, yolk) in grease wool.
*   **Yield:** The percentage of clean wool remaining after scouring.
*   **Yolk:** A combination of wool grease and dried perspiration.
*   **Averages:** The average yield of fine wool in Texas is 50 percent. High-yielding fleeces are those yielding over 53%; low-yielding fleeces are under 45%.

### 4. Secondary Judging Characteristics
*   **Soundness (Strength):** Fiber should be strong throughout. "Tender wool" is weak along the entire fiber, while a "break" is a specific weak spot where fibers snap when stretched.
*   **Purity:** Freedom from kemp (opaque, brittle fibers) and black/colored fibers.
*   **Character:** Refers to general appearance and **crimp** (natural waviness). Distinct, even crimp indicates strength and less manufacturing breakage.
*   **Color:** Bright white to creamy white is preferred. Stains and dark colors limit the wool's use to dark fabrics.

## Part 2: Mohair Evaluation and Grading

Mohair, produced by Angora goats, follows similar evaluation principles to wool but with specific differences in grading and preferred traits.

### 1. Mohair Grades
Grades are based on fineness, not the age of the goat, though older goats generally produce coarser hair.

| Grade Name | Spinning Count |
| :--- | :--- |
| Kid 1 | 36’s – 40’s |
| Kid 2 | 30’s – 32’s |
| Adult 1 | 26’s – 28’s |
| Adult 2 | 22’s – 24’s |
| Adult 3 | 18’s – 20’s |
| Adult 4 | Coarser than 18’s |

### 2. Key Mohair Evaluation Factors
*   **Staple Length:** Highly important for fleece weight. Ideal growth is 1 inch per month (6 inches for a 6-month shearing).
*   **Luster:** The brightness or shininess of the fiber. High luster is highly preferred.
*   **Softness (Handle):** Harshness is undesirable; softness is required for high-quality fabrics.
*   **Condition:** Ideally, shrinkage should be approximately 15 percent. Excessive natural or artificial oil (spraying) is discriminated against.
*   **Purity:** Freedom from kemp (chalky white hairs) and colored fibers. Kemp is most often found on the back and thighs.

## Part 3: Judging and Scoring Mechanics

Judging contests typically involve two components: **Classification** (grading individual samples) and **Placing** (ranking groups of four fleeces).

### 1. Wool Judging Scorecard
| Characteristic | Points |
| :--- | :--- |
| Estimated Clean Yield | 35 |
| Staple Length | 25 |
| Quality (Fineness) | 10 |
| Soundness (Strength) | 10 |
| Purity | 10 |
| Character and Color | 10 |
| **Total** | **100** |

### 2. Mohair Judging Scorecard
| Characteristic | Points |
| :--- | :--- |
| Fineness and Uniformity | 40 |
| Staple Length | 20 |
| Condition or Yield | 10 |
| Character | 10 |
| Purity | 10 |
| Luster | 5 |
| Softness | 5 |
| **Total** | **100** |

### 3. Commercial vs. Breeding Classes
*   **Commercial Classes:** Placed on the basis of the greatest economic return to the producer (primarily pounds of clean wool). Vegetable matter and strength are heavily weighted as they affect manufacturing.
*   **Breeding Classes:** Focus on highly heritable traits. Strength and vegetable matter are often disregarded as they are environmentally influenced. Increased emphasis is placed on purity and breed character.

## Short-Answer Practice Quiz

**1. What are the two main factors used to determine the grade of a wool fleece?**
*Answer: Fineness and length.*

**2. Define the term "yolk" in relation to wool.**
*Answer: The combination of wool grease and dried perspiration found in the fleece.*

**3. What is the difference between "tender" wool and a "break"?**
*Answer: Tender wool is weak throughout the entire fiber length; a break is a specific weak spot where all fibers snap squarely at the same point.*

**4. How many yards of yarn are contained in a standard worsted "hank"?**
*Answer: 560 yards.*

**5. Which grade of mohair is typically the finest and highest in value?**
*Answer: Kid 1 (36’s – 40’s).*

**6. What is the ideal growth rate for mohair staple length?**
*Answer: One inch per month.*

**7. Why are black or pigmented fibers considered a serious defect in white wool?**
*Answer: They limit the use of the wool to the manufacture of dark-colored fabrics, as they cannot be dyed light colors.*

**8. In a wool judging contest, how many percentage points of yield are required to equal one pound of grease weight?**
*Answer: 10 percentage points.*

## Glossary of Important Terms

*   **Break:** A specific point in the fiber length that is abnormally weak, usually caused by fever, sudden feed changes, or severe weather.
*   **Breech (Britch):** Coarse, hair-like fibers found on the lower hind legs and dock of some sheep.
*   **Character:** The general appearance of fiber, specifically the presence of distinct crimp in wool or well-defined locks in mohair.
*   **Clip:** The total wool or mohair produced by one flock in a single season.
*   **Crimp:** The natural waviness or curl of the fiber.
*   **Fleece:** The wool or mohair produced by a single animal at one shearing.
*   **Kemp:** An opaque, brittle, chalky white hair-like fiber that is a serious defect because it does not dye well and breaks easily.
*   **Noils:** Short fibers removed from long ones during the combing process.
*   **Purity:** The absence of off-type fibers such as kemp, hair, or colored fibers.
*   **Shrinkage:** The weight lost when raw wool is scoured, expressed as a percentage of the grease weight.
*   **Sorting:** The process of breaking up individual fleeces into various grades.
*   **Tags:** Heavy, dung-covered locks of fiber.
*   **Top:** A continuous band of long wool fibers that have been made parallel through combing, with noils removed.
*   **Yield:** The percentage of clean fiber remaining after the scouring process.
